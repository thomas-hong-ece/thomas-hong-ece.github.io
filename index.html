Undergraduate research assistant on the MITEC process team at Ohio State's Nanotech West cleanroom. I run full photolithography cycles on 20+ silicon wafers per run: spin coating resist, mask alignment and exposure, and the soft and hard bakes in between. Five-plus months in, zero contamination incidents, which is the number that actually matters in a shared fab.

The other half is metrology. After exposure I check pattern fidelity on a Dektak XT stylus profilometer and under optical microscopy, looking for process drift before it reaches etch, where a bad wafer gets expensive. Across 20+ runs that has cut reprocessing. I also rewrote the process docs and tuned resist thickness, bake timing and alignment so new researchers hit consistent critical dimensions faster. Onboarding time is down about 30%.

24-hour makeathon build (MakeOHI/O 2026): a live demonstration of dynamic line rating. Nichrome wire stands in for a conductor; an Arduino Nano ESP32 reads conductor temperature (DS18B20), ambient temperature and humidity, and a VL53L4CD time-of-flight LiDAR tracking sag off a weighted target. A small fan simulates wind so we could show forced-convection cooling pulling the sag back. As the wire heats, it expands, and the system watches it happen.

The hard part was physical: every sensor mount melted off past 50 °C until heat-shrink tubing held at 4am. Logged 700+ measurements; analysis matched IEEE 738 thermal expansion models within ~5% error, and the wind-cooling demo was a variable most competing teams didn't model. Team: Leslie Torkornoo, Anzony Santos, Mohamed Moujahid.

Bench: Arduino Nano ESP32 · VL53L4CD ToF LiDAR · DS18B20 · temp/humidity sensor · CSV pipeline · Python analysis  |  Build photos & write-up →

IgnisKey: Custom Heated Key Switch · Boeing-Sponsored Internship

5 REVS
TEAM
LEAD OF 4
REVIEWED BY
BOEING ENGINEERS

IgnisKey started as a Boeing-sponsored student project with a simple brief: a keyboard that warms your fingers. The catch is that a heating element only helps if it sits right under the key, and a normal key switch has nowhere to put one. So the switch itself became the part we had to design.

My answer was a two-piece housing with a thermistor seated above the PCB inside the switch cavity, so the heat travels up the stem into a carbon fiber keycap. Carbon fiber is a poor conductor through its thickness but a good one along the fibers, which means heat arriving at one small point on the stem spreads out across the whole key instead of making a hot spot. That one material choice solved both the comfort problem and the burn-risk problem at once.

I led a team of four through five prototypes between February and April 2025. Each round we cut something out of the PCB assembly that a failure-mode review had flagged as unnecessary, so builds got faster every revision. We presented a working prototype and a formal design review to Boeing engineers in Ridley Park on schedule, and they pointed to our trade-off documentation as the model for teams after us.

Bench: mechanical design (Onshape) · PCB design · thermal packaging · materials selection · design review · team lead of 4
